Analysis window:

 supplied IPL and T20I data, seasons 2022–2026. The 2026 season may be incomplete.

This analysis defines pressure from the live innings state before a delivery, then compares each player's pressured performance with their ordinary performance. Scores are standardized within format-season pools,

include match-to-match consistency, and are shrunk toward the field midpoint when evidence is limited. The dashboard recalculates these lists from the same shared scoring module.

Exclusion policy.

 The submitted ranking uses only the supplied data. It applies no availability or nationality filter: every player in the supplied IPL and T20I records who meets the sample

thresholds is ranked, and the problem statement does not ask for any exclusion. The ranking with exclusions is shown as an alternative, with the same metrics, in "Sensitivity and exclusions".

The alternative removes three groups of players before scoring: 53 players who appear for Pakistan in the supplied T20I data (Pakistan T20I team representation is the nationality proxy), five

players with confirmed IPL playing retirements (Kieron Pollard, Dwayne Bravo, Ambati Rayudu, Dinesh Karthik, Ravichandran Ashwin), and Mohit Sharma. It replaces 2 of the ten batters, 3 of the

ten bowlers and 1 of the ten all-rounders. To switch to it, see "Switch the exclusions on".

 How to read the undocumented 

other

any candidate ran. The 

other

not committed beforehand. Both tests kept the submitted method, so the lists in this report are unchanged. The tests are in "Evidence for the two decisions".

Pressure is a match state

A delivery is classified from the score, wickets, and legal balls remaining immediately before it. If multiple rules apply, it is still counted once.

01 / FINISH PHASE

30 balls or fewer remain

Captures the final five overs of either innings, when each

scoring or stopping opportunity has immediate leverage.

02 / WICKET SQUEEZE

Five down, time short

At least five wickets have fallen, 48 legal balls have

elapsed, and 72 or fewer remain.

03 / CHASE SQUEEZE

The asking rate bites

Second innings, after 18 legal balls: required rate is 9.5+,

or 7.5+ and at least 2.5 above the current rate.

Why these cutoffs.

 They are fixed judgement calls, and no procedure in this project searched for them or tuned them against player results. Thirty balls is the last five overs of a 20-over innings (the same 30 balls

remaining when the innings is shortened by revised overs). Five wickets is half of the batting side's wickets. 48 balls played and at most 72 remaining mean eight overs gone and twelve or fewer left; in a full 20-over

innings those two conditions are the same (48 + 72 = 120), so they differ only in shortened innings. The required rate is read only after 18 balls (three overs) because it swings widely over the first few balls. A required

rate of 9.5 is above what bowlers concede in this data (9.26 runs per over on pressure deliveries and 8.31 on ordinary ones), so a side that needs it must score faster than the usual rate. The second test, a required rate of

at least 7.5 and at least 2.5 above the current rate, catches chases that fall behind a moderate target; 7.5 and 2.5 are round-number choices. The sensitivity section varies the sample thresholds, the exclusions and the

other

 extras rule. It does not vary these pressure cutoffs, so how far the lists would move under different cutoffs has not been measured.

How the index is built

Only IPL and T20I competitions with a readable season from 2022 onward are included. A season label is read as its leading four-digit year, so a competition labelled 

2022/23

 belongs to season 2022. Super-over

innings (innings 3 and 4) are skipped. The in-scope data contains 

837 matches

 and 

193,988 deliveries

: 

81,806 pressure

 (42.2%) and 

112,182 ordinary

. The supplied ball-by-ball file has 200,999 raw rows; the difference

is excluded competitions, skipped super-over innings, or out-of-scope records, not missing rows. The submitted ranking applies no player exclusions: every player in the supplied data who meets the thresholds below is

ranked. The alternative ranking, which applies the exclusions described in "Alternative: top 10 lists with the exclusions applied", is reported under "Sensitivity and exclusions". Players are analyzed by format and season. A

player-format-season segment must have at least 

60 pressure balls

, 

8 matches containing pressure batting/bowling

, and 

60 ordinary balls

. After format-season roll-up, 66 batter profiles and 136 bowler profiles

qualify; 12 players qualify in both disciplines for the all-rounder list. The dashboard also offers 36-ball/5-match and 90-ball/10-match sensitivity settings; their results are in "Sensitivity and exclusions".

The calculation joins 

ball_by_ball.csv

 to 

matches.csv

 and 

competitions.csv

. IPL is identified by the competition name; T20I is identified by the competition format. Other competitions carrying a

generic T20 label and records with no readable season are excluded. Deliveries are processed in file order. The current delivery is classified first; only then are its score, wicket, and legal-ball effects applied to the innings

state. Revised-overs fields greater than 20 are interpreted as legal-ball counts (for example, 114 means 19 overs); smaller nonzero values are treated as overs. Revised targets are used when present; otherwise the innings

limit is 20 overs and the second-innings target is one more than the first-innings total. Wides, no-balls and the 

other

 extras type do not count as legal balls, and wides do not count as balls faced. Batter runs come

from 

runs

. Byes and leg-byes are not charged to bowlers. Because reliable run amounts for these extras are not present in the supplied fields, their runs are omitted from reconstructed chase totals. This makes chase

required-rate pressure an approximation.

Only 

ball_by_ball.csv

, 

matches.csv

 and 

competitions.csv

 are read by the analysis. 

teams.csv

, 

playing_eleven.csv

 and 

competition_team_player_mapping.csv

 are not

needed for the scores: team names come from 

matches.csv

, and the ranking does not use line-ups. The data pack has no 

players.csv

; player ids and names come from the ball-by-ball columns. All 20 ranked

batters and bowlers appear in the mapping file with one id each, equal to the id in the ball-by-ball file.

Batting inputs

Higher pressure strike rate, strike-rate improvement over the player's ordinary baseline, lower dismissal

rate per 100 balls, and greater positive-match share.

Bowling inputs

Lower pressure economy, improvement over the player's ordinary economy, more pressure wickets per

100 legal balls, and greater positive-match share.

Weights and shrinkage

Each direction-aligned input is a z-score against eligible players in that format-season stratum. Each

stratum uses its own players (

own-pool

, the default of 

POOL_METHOD

 in 

scoring.mjs

); three

other pool rules were tested for the small T20I pools and not adopted (see "Evidence for the two

decisions").

0.35 × output + 0.30 × baseline change

+ 0.20 × outcome + 0.15 × match consistency

Reliability is √((balls/(balls+60)) × (matches/(matches+6))). Index = clip(50 + 10 × weighted score ×

reliability, 0, 100). The unshrunk field midpoint is 50. A player's segment scores are combined using

pressure-ball volume as the weight. Reliability is a heuristic shrinkage factor, not a confidence interval.

All-rounder method

All-rounders must qualify for both discipline lists under the same format, season, and sample filters. Composite index = 0.50 × batting index + 0.50 × bowling index. Each component already includes its own reliability

adjustment. The dashboard displays both component indexes and sample sizes alongside the composite. This is a dual-discipline performance ranking, not an official player-role label.

Assumption used for the submitted lists.

 A delivery with 

extras_type = other

 (264 rows in the supplied file) is read as a no-ball variant: it is not one of the six legal balls of an over, and the bowler is charged

one penalty run, which also counts in the innings score used for the required rate. The tests in "Evidence for the two decisions" support the first part strongly and the second part moderately.

Evidence.

 

extras1_runs

 is 5 on all 264 rows (wide is 2, 

Overstep

 is 1, ordinary balls 6). Batter runs are recorded on 136 of the 264 rows (26 sixes, 42 fours), against 337 of 576 

Overstep

 rows and none of

7,714 wides. 254 overs contain an 

other

 row. Counted as a legal ball, 243 of them would hold more than six legal balls (all 216 of those that are not the last over of an innings); counted as an illegal ball, 243 hold

exactly six (216 of 216). Overs with no 

other

 row hold exactly six legal balls in 30,406 of 30,410 finished overs. No column gives the value of the 

other

 extra: 

runs

 is the batter's runs on every extras type, so the

one-run penalty cannot be read from the data directly. An exact comparison with the official margins has little power, because the file holds no innings totals: among 11 comparable matches decided by runs and without

an 

other

 row, the margin equals the difference between the innings totals in only 5, and the one comparable match with an 

other

 row (India won by 61 runs) gives a difference of 61 with the penalty run and 60

without it. A regression of the margins on the count of each extras type is the test that can tell the readings apart. It puts the value of an 

other

 extra at 1.11 runs (95% interval 0.66 to 1.56), against 0.90 for the

Overstep

 control, so the penalty run stays.

Re-rating tests.

 Each test re-rates every player with 

scoring.mjs

 on changed data (fixed seed; details in 

sensitivity_data.json

, 

rankStability

). Leave-one-out removes one other eligible player of

the same kind at a time. Pool deletion removes a random 20% of the eligible players who are not in the submitted top ten, 1,000 times, so it changes only who is in the pools. The bootstrap resamples each player's match

records, 1,000 times, so it also moves the player's own results. The tables give the share of runs in which a submitted player is still in the top ten. The class is fragile below 50%, moderate from 50% to under 80% and

stable at 80% or more, using the unrounded share.

Decision 2: the small T20I pools.

players (2023 and 2026); with the exclusions applied five batter pools hold 5 to 10. A z-score inside such a pool moves a lot when one player is added or removed.

Criterion, written first.

 The criterion and the candidates were committed as

applied mechanically, once, with no change of parameters. Two settings were tested: S1, the submitted lists (no exclusions), and S2, the same data with the exclusions on, where thin pools are more common. Batters and

own-pool (primary, submitted):

C2 shrunk:

C3 adjacent-fallback:

 a thin pool is standardised against the season before, the same season and the season after; if that is still thin, against all seasons of the format.

Consistency, data checks, and limits

Positive-match share is the fraction of a player's pressure-exposed matches that pass a test against the player's pooled ordinary rates. A batter pressure match is positive when 

(pressure SR − usual SR) −

2 × (pressure outs/ball − usual outs/ball) > 0

. A bowler match is positive when 

(usual economy − pressure economy) + 1.5 × (pressure wickets/100 balls −

usual wickets/100 balls) > 0

.

Run-outs, retired hurt/out (including the source spelling 

Retire Out

), timed out, handled-the-ball, and obstructing-the-field dismissals are not bowler wickets. A non-striker run-out contributes to that batter's

dismissal rate but not balls faced. Labels are normalized for spaces and spelling variations, including 

RunOut

 and the source typo 

Obstracting the field

.

The extras fields are inconsistent: 

extras1_runs

 is 6 on ordinary deliveries and has categorical-looking values on tagged extras. The analysis does not treat it as runs. One-run penalties are added for wides, no-

balls and the undocumented 

other

 type (264 rows, treated as a no-ball); bye/leg-bye totals are unavailable and omitted, so reconstructed chase rates are approximate.

The supplied boundary flags disagree with 

runs

 on 23 deliveries; batter runs use 

runs

, not the flags. Revised-overs values above 20 are legal-ball counts (114 means 19 overs). Revised targets/overs are used

when present, but full Duckworth-Lewis par scores and ball-level win probability are not available.

Sample filters and shrinkage reduce, but do not eliminate, small-sample and selection effects. The displayed reliability is not a confidence interval.

Format-season standardization helps separate scoring environments, but IPL and T20I roles and opposition quality are not fully interchangeable. Only strata meeting eligibility contribute to a player's roll-up.

Player indexes are not adjusted for opposition, venue or batting position, so differences in match context between players are not removed.

Scores are rounded to one decimal before ranking, so adjacent players can be very close. Players with the same rounded index are ordered by pressure balls, then by name (all-rounders: by the lower of their two

component indexes, then by name). In the submitted lists, the only tie at the edge of a top ten is Namboori Tilak Varma (10th) and Jos Buttler (11th), 53.1 each; Tilak Varma has 1,048 pressure balls against 344. In the

alternative lists the tied pairs are Sunil Narine and Umran Malik (61.3 each; Narine has 684 pressure balls against 97) and Rasikh Salam and Benjamin Lister (56.3 each; 137 against 78). Ordering by the unrounded

index gives the same order in all three cases.

Pressure thresholds are transparent proxies, not direct measures of psychology; they may miss rivalry, knockout stakes, crowd conditions, or a close first-innings situation. Rankings are not causal estimates.

Small T20I pools: with no exclusions and the default thresholds, T20I format-season pools hold 5 to 12 eligible batters (12 to 17 in IPL seasons) and 12 to 40 eligible bowlers (32 to 36 in IPL seasons). Only the 2023

and 2026 batter pools hold 10 or fewer players. Mark Chapman gets 76% of his pressure balls from the 2023 pool of five. With the pools of fewer than 8 players dropped, Namboori Tilak Varma (10th) falls to 15th,

and with the pools of fewer than 12 players dropped, Fakhar Zaman and Mark Chapman are not eligible. Namboori Tilak Varma is in the top ten in 25% of pool-deletion runs. The numbers are in "Small T20I pools".

Three alternative pool rules (standardising a thin pool against all seasons of the format, against the neighbouring seasons, or with shrinkage) were tested and none met the pre-registered criterion, so the method and

the submitted lists were not changed. Under two of them Mark Chapman (7th) would be first, so his place depends on the pool rule ("Evidence for the two decisions").
